Mesh networking: one app connection manages every fixture¶

Status: design (this document). Nothing is implemented yet. Phase 1 (the application backhaul) is the critical path for the radiolarians install.

Owner’s request¶

“It’s time to start building mesh networking :) We should support two mesh backhauls. One is UWB and the other is ESP-NOW style P2P. Since we have our custom C6 netstack, we should build ESP-NOW (or something like it) in the netstack. This mesh backhaul should enable distributing uniforms and effects programs, such that a single connection (BLE or WiFi) from the app can allow management of all devices in the mesh. The mesh networking needs to support assigning devices to groups and freezing membership, in addition to free association (devices arriving at an existing mesh and being automatically peered in).”

Follow-up directions from the owner, folded into this design:

  1. The UWB mesh in fughilli/indoor_localization (the “swarm”) is the basis. Splanc adopts its model, frame formats and transport. The 802.11 backhaul carries the same mesh layer.

  2. Realtime localization (IMU + ranging fusion, as in indoor_localization) comes later.

  3. BLE ranging on the C6 should be evaluated, including a self-built Channel Sounding-like ranging on top of our own radio work.

  4. Priority: the application backhaul ships first, for the radiolarians deployment. Their fixtures are heavy and static, so the existing room localization is enough to provision the install. Ranging, fusion and channel sounding are later, independent tracks.

  5. Owner answers: up to 6 radiolarians, each a P4 + C6 driving WLEDs over DDP; BLE → mesh for sites without Wi-Fi; three membership modes (open, closed, locked).

  6. The swarm code is ported into Splanc (indoor_localization is public), not shared as a dependency.

  7. Radiolarians details: WLEDs on the P4’s Ethernet; fixtures a few metres apart in metal enclosures; they self-configure; admin over BLE, Wi-Fi or the bridge; per-device Wi-Fi OTA is enough; a closed mesh unlocked with a kiosk-style admin secret; any fixture is a gateway; Splanc’s own frame format, no Espressif OUI.

1. Summary¶

  • One mesh layer, several backhauls. The mesh layer is the swarm’s swarm_net crate (swarm/net in indoor_localization): managed flooding with dedup and TTL, want-ack unicast, authenticated generation-numbered state, and a Deluge-style page protocol for blobs. Splanc adds a security envelope, membership and groups, and Splanc ports on top. Backhauls plug in under a small Backhaul trait.

  • Phase 1 backhaul: 802.11 vendor action frames in our netstack, with Splanc’s own framing (a Splanc CID, no Espressif OUI). The netstack already sends raw management frames (ns_mac_send) and receives every frame on the channel. Mesh frames share the AP’s channel when associated, and a fixed mesh channel when not.

  • The gateway is whichever fixture the app (or bridge) is connected to, over BLE or wss. It relays wrapped ClientMessages to any member, group or the whole mesh, and aggregates acks and status.

  • Effects (.fxb, at most 4 KiB, FX_MAX_BYTES in firmware/player_app/ffi.rs) travel as content-addressed blobs over the page protocol. Uniforms, brightness and crossfade travel as small flooded packets.

  • Mesh time is disciplined from beacons. The app’s SetTimeBase epoch is mapped into mesh time, so apply_at_ms (docs/design/synced-show.md) lands a relayed change on the same frame on every member.

  • Membership: a mesh has an id, a key and an epoch, and one of three modes set from the app. Open: any Splanc device can follow the show (time, scenes, effects) as a guest, with no secret, but can never manage it. Closed: devices holding the site secret from provisioning join as members. Locked: only devices the owner approved; removal rotates the key. Groups target messages. Administration is unlocked per connection with a kiosk-style admin secret kept in the app and exportable to other admins.

  • BLE or Wi-Fi in. Sites without Wi-Fi are first-class: the phone connects over BLE to any fixture, which gateways to a mesh running in a radio-up, no-AP mode.

  • Radiolarians (up to 6 P4 + C6 fixtures driving WLEDs over DDP): the C6 front end is the mesh node and gateway, the P4 the endpoint.

  • Later tracks: UWB (a hardware revision: no current Splanc board has UWB), ranging plus IMU fusion, and a research track on CS-like phase ranging using the C6’s undocumented raw I/Q capture.

The parts Splanc adopts:

  • MeshPacket header (≤ 24 B): origin u16, id u16, dest (absent = broadcast), hop_limit, hop_start, flags (want-ack, is-ack), attempt, port, payload (≤ 88 B on UWB).

  • Managed flooding (swarm/net/src/mesh.rs MeshConfig): hop limit 3; dedup on (origin, id, attempt) over a 64-entry ring; relay after 0–3 of the node’s own slots, sooner the weaker the RSSI; cancel a pending relay on overhearing it; want-ack unicast retried 3 times, 1 s apart.

  • Generations and catch-up (“Live settings”): every swarm-wide change carries a host generation; Summary.generation in each node’s beacon lets a late joiner fetch what it missed.

  • Page protocol (swarm/net/src/update.rs): 4 KB pages, 96 B chunks, NACK bitmaps to the most advanced neighbour, overheard-request suppression, the canary rule.

  • Seam: Net::on_mesh(t_us, bytes, rssi, flash), Net::on_my_slot(), Net::poll(t_us), Net::next_tx(t_us, out, flash) -> Option<(FrameKind, usize)>, Net::send(dest, port, payload, want_ack), plus trait Flash. There is no radio trait yet. Splanc adds one (§3.2).

What does not carry over as is:

  • The swarm authenticates only settings (4-byte truncated HMAC-SHA256) and firmware (ECDSA). Reports and acks are unauthenticated. Lights in someone’s home need every control frame authenticated (§7.5).

  • Membership is implicit (a node’s slot is its rank among heard ids). There is no join, no groups, no modes (§7).

  • Time sync is coarse (superframes align to the lowest id’s POLL). Splanc needs ms-level shared time for apply_at (§6).

  • The 88 B payload is a UWB budget. The 802.11 backhaul allows more (§5.2).

3.1 Where the code lives: port the swarm into Splanc¶

The owner’s call: port swarm/net and swarm/proto into Splanc as Splanc code. No shared repo, no Bazel dependency on indoor_localization (which is public now).

  • Where: firmware/mesh/swarm/ (the ported swarm_net crate, kept close to upstream) and firmware/mesh/ (the Splanc layer on top: backhauls, envelope, membership, ports). The proto goes to shared/protocol/proto/swarm_wire.proto next to ledmapper.proto, generated with Splanc’s existing protobuf tooling (micropb on firmware; pbview is already in oss/pbview).

  • Provenance: a header comment in each ported file and a firmware/mesh/swarm/PROVENANCE.md naming the source (fughilli/indoor_localization, swarm/net and swarm/proto at 5a906e7823f2d1c13af30dfa2a3b1b887616d0f1) and listing what Splanc changed.

  • License: swarm/ is the owner’s own code, so it comes in under Splanc’s AGPL-3.0. (Even taken as GPL-3.0 from the repo root, GPL-3.0 §13 allows combining it with AGPL-3.0 code.) The GPL-derived DW3000 driver (libs/dw3000) is not ported; a UWB driver is a later, separate question (§11).

  • From then on Splanc owns its copy. Fixes worth sharing go back to indoor_localization by hand; the golden vectors (swarm/proto/testdata) come along so the wire format stays checkable against the swarm’s.

Changes made while porting (M1 unless noted):

  1. A Backhaul-friendly entry point: on_rx(t_us, frame, RxMeta) and a slot tick that a backhaul without TDMA can drive from a timer.

  2. A per-instance MAX_PAYLOAD (88 on UWB, larger on 802.11; §5.2) instead of one constant.

  3. Blob kinds on the page protocol: Summary/FwRequest/FwData gain a kind (firmware, effect) and a content id, so the same Updater serves effects (§5.5). This can wait for M6.

  4. A hook for the security envelope (§7.5) around LinkFrame.

  5. Builds for riscv32imac (C6) and the P4 target, plus the host tests, in Splanc’s CI. The crate is already no_std; its crypto backend switches to Splanc’s (the netstack’s AES, mbedTLS for SHA-256 and P-256).

3.2 The backhaul seam¶

/// One radio (or link) that carries mesh frames. Implementations own framing,
/// channel, rate and pacing; the mesh layer sees opaque frames.
pub trait Backhaul {
    /// Largest mesh frame (after the security envelope) this backhaul carries.
    fn mtu(&self) -> usize;
    /// Queue a frame. `dst = None` broadcasts. Never blocks; Busy is back-pressure.
    fn send(&mut self, frame: &[u8], dst: Option<NodeId>) -> Result<(), Busy>;
    /// Next received frame, with its metadata.
    fn recv(&mut self, buf: &mut [u8]) -> Option<RxMeta>;
    /// Whether this node may transmit now (TDMA slot on UWB, always on 802.11).
    fn may_send(&self, now_us: i64) -> bool;
}

pub struct RxMeta {
    pub len: usize,
    pub rssi_dbm: i8,
    pub rx_us: i64,          // hardware RX timestamp on this node's clock
    pub src_mac: [u8; 6],    // link address, for unicast replies
    pub backhaul: BackhaulId,
}

The mesh layer drives swarm_net from these calls. On UWB, on_my_slot comes from the TDMA schedule. On 802.11 there is no TDMA: a 5 ms virtual slot timer drives the relay delays, and the MAC’s CSMA handles collisions.

A node with two backhauls relays across them. The dedup ring makes that safe: a packet seen on both is relayed once per backhaul at most.

3.3 Addressing¶

  • Node id: u16, derived from the factory MAC like the swarm’s IdFromMac(). Collisions are possible (16 bits of a 48-bit MAC). The gateway detects them from the members’ full MACs in MeshStatus (§5.4) and the app reassigns one id, persisted on the device.

  • Device id in the app stays the MAC (welcome MAC, deviceTrust.ts). The app maps MAC to node id from status reports.

  • Targets: one node, a list of up to 8 nodes, a group (u8), or all.

Why Public Action (verified in Linux 6.12, net/wireless/mlme.c and net/mac80211/):

  • TX. cfg80211_mlme_mgmt_tx skips its per-interface checks only for category 4; for any other category a station may address nothing but its own AP. mac80211’s ieee80211_mgmt_tx then sends on-channel when associated, or off-channel (remain-on-channel) when not.

  • RX. ieee80211_rx_h_action drops every non-Public, non-self-protected action frame whose sender has no station entry (RX_DROP_U_ACTION_UNKNOWN_SRC) before userspace can see it; ieee80211_accept_frame explicitly accepts Public Action even when the BSSID doesn’t match (“used for P2P and location updates”).

  • Action code 9 is the standard vendor-specific Public Action. The Wi-Fi Alliance’s users of 4/9 (P2P, DPP, NAN) are keyed by its OUI 50:6F:9A, so our CID cannot collide, and none of wpa_supplicant’s station-mode registrations is a prefix of ours. In AP mode hostapd registers all of category 4 (and all of 127), so a host that runs hostapd on the same interface receives mesh frames another way (a monitor interface, or the brcmfmac path in mesh-pi-transport.md); the category does not change that.

  • brcmfmac (the Pi’s own radio) refuses both categories on the normal path and accepts both through its firmware passthrough, so it does not decide the question.

The identifier. A vendor-specific action frame starts with an OUI or a CID (an IEEE Company ID: 24 bits with the local, “X”, bit set, so a CID never collides with any assigned OUI).

  • Ship with a registered CID. IEEE RA sells CIDs for a modest fee; registering one before the first install is the clean answer (an open question for the owner, §17).

  • Until then, use a CID-format placeholder (0x0A 0x5C 0x4D, local bit set): it cannot collide with an OUI-assigned company. A later CID swap is one constant plus a version bump.

  • TX: ns_mac_send(frame, len, queue) already submits unprotected management frames (Auth, Assoc use it). Mesh frames go through the same path, after tx_pace_before(), so they never arm on top of a frame still on air. Broadcast frames get no ACK; unicast frames to a peer’s MAC get the peer’s hardware auto-ACK (mac_own_bssid keeps own-MAC valid).

  • RX: netstack_loop drains ns_mac_recv. A mesh frame is identified before the BSSID gate: rx[0] == 0xd0, category 4, action 9, the Splanc CID, kind 0x01, then the envelope magic. It is handed to the mesh layer and never to the STA path.

  • RX metadata: decode RSSI and the RX timestamp from the 92-byte RX vector (RX_VECTOR_HDR). The layout is undocumented. Spike M3a finds the fields on silicon by comparing against the vendor wifi_pkt_rx_ctrl_t in promiscuous mode.

  • Rate: start with 1 Mb/s DSSS for broadcast (robust, longest range) and measure 6 Mb/s OFDM. A 250-byte frame is ~2.2 ms at 1 Mb/s and ~0.4 ms at 6 Mb/s. The pacing formula in tx_pace_after must use the rate actually sent.

  • Reference bytes: pi/mesh_radio/src/frame.rs (golden_public_vendor_frame) is the exact frame the netstack must emit for a given header and envelope; the Pi transport (PR #533) is built against it.

  • Size: 802.11 allows management frames up to 2304 B. We start with 250 B bodies (short airtime at 1 Mb/s, fits every buffer on the path) and raise it only if measurements say it pays (M14).

4.3 Coexistence with BLE¶

The BLE gateway (the app on BLE, the fixture relaying to the mesh) is a Phase 1 path: sites without Wi-Fi have no other way in. So the gateway’s one radio must serve a BLE connection and the mesh channel at once.

Rules for the BLE gateway:

  • Ask for a ≥ 50 ms connection interval once the GATT session is up and the mesh is active (an L2CAP connection parameter update from the peripheral). Phones usually accept it. Control traffic from the app is small, so 50 ms costs it nothing visible; apply_at hides the extra latency.

  • Narrow the window. ±7 ms is generous for a connection event that carries one or two packets (~1–3 ms of air). Measure, then try ±3 ms: at 50 ms that is ~88 % for the mesh.

  • Send mesh frames outside the BLE window. The gateway knows the window (the same phase math as coex_timer_cb), so it queues mesh TX until the Wi-Fi phase. A relay waiting 0–3 virtual slots of 5 ms fits between windows.

  • Only the gateway pays. Other members have no BLE connection and listen full time. A broadcast the gateway misses during a BLE window is relayed by others; replies to the gateway are unicast, so the sender’s MAC retries them (M3a checks that retries work on our TX path).

  • Mesh traffic is light (§10.3), so it does not starve BLE.

On P4 + C6 boards the C6 front end owns BLE (docs/design/p4-c6-split.md §2.1) and has no render task, so the gateway logic runs at full priority there.

4.4 Spike before committing (M3a)¶

Two C6s on a rig, with and without an AP:

  1. Broadcast a vendor action frame from one netstack image; receive it on the other while that one is associated to the rig AP. Confirms the RX path and that the AP association survives.

  2. Unicast with hardware ACK: check retries on a missed ACK, and whether the MAC retries management frames at all on the direct-submission path.

  3. Decode RSSI and the RX timestamp from the RX vector.

  4. Measure delivery while the gateway holds a BLE connection.

Exit: frames flow both ways in both cases with ≥ 95 % delivery at one hop, and the RX timestamp is found.

4.5 Antennas and metal enclosures¶

The radiolarians sit a few metres apart with clear line of sight, but inside metal enclosures, with people walking between them. A closed metal box is close to a Faraday cage: a module’s PCB antenna inside it can lose tens of dB, and the loss depends on seams, holes and where the antenna sits. The same antenna serves the mesh and the phone’s BLE link.

4.6 PHY rate: adapted per node, gated by measurement¶

The owner does not pick a rate: the measurements decide (Q12). Every node adapts its broadcast rate on its own, and R0 and the HITL scenarios gate what ships.

Adaptation (modelled in meshsim, RateCtl; firmware in M3):

  • Ladder: 1 Mb/s DSSS → 6 → 12 → 24 Mb/s OFDM. A node starts at the most robust rate.

  • Feedback is the share of its frames that its solid neighbours still receive. On hardware this comes from per-neighbour heard counts in their adverts (up to 8 neighbours, ~16 B) and from MAC ACKs on unicasts.

    • A neighbour is solid when the most robust rate reaches it with a 10 dB margin.

    • Marginal neighbours are left to relays: holding the whole mesh at 1 Mb/s for them costs more airtime than it saves.

  • Rules, per 1 s window:

    • Step up after two windows at ≥ 90 %.

    • Step down below 80 %.

    • A failed probe doubles the hold-off before the next one (up to 32 s).

    • On a busy channel (> 50 % of the window sensed busy), poor delivery means collisions, not range, so step up (shorter frames).

  • Live control is never paused for adaptation: a rate change takes effect on the next frame.

5.5 Effect programs: content-addressed blobs¶

  1. The app sends SubmitEffect to the gateway as today (BLE uses UploadChunk reassembly).

  2. The gateway stores it (existing path), computes blob_id = SHA-256(fxb)[0..8] and starts serving it on the page protocol (swarm/net/src/update.rs, generalised with a blob kind). A 4 KiB effect is one page of up to 43 chunks of 96 B (on 802.11, fewer and larger chunks fit one 200 B payload).

  3. A MeshControl{set_effect by blob_id, apply_at} tells targets what to load. A target that lacks the blob requests its missing chunks (NACK bitmap) from the most advanced neighbour. It writes chunks into the effect store under effect_id (littlefs), not into RAM.

  4. Once complete and the SHA-256 matches, the member loads it. A scheduled set_effect applies at its apply_at; one that arrives before the blob is complete is held until it completes (a late member then starts late, as on wss today).

The canary rule (serve only what you validated) maps to: serve only blobs whose hash checks.

5.6 Scene state and late joiners¶

The swarm’s “late joiners catch up” rule, applied to looks. Each group has a scene: active effect blob id per deck, committed uniforms, brightness, crossfade, with a generation the gateway increments on every committed change. Members put (group, generation) pairs in their BEACON summary. A member that hears a newer generation for one of its groups asks that neighbour for the scene (a few packets) and applies it. A fixture powered on after the show started is showing the room’s look within seconds.

Live (uncommitted) moves such as a fader drag are not part of the scene; the final committed value is.

5.7 Versioning¶

  • Envelope version for the frame format; LinkFrame.ver for the swarm wire format.

  • Mesh features are advertised in welcome.features (mesh, mesh_gateway) and in MeshStatus. The gateway does not send a member an arm its firmware version lacks.

  • A member with an unknown envelope version is reported in the gateway’s list as “incompatible”, so the app can offer an update over a direct connection.

5.8 Two-tier distribution tree: UWB to relays, 802.11 to fixtures¶

The owner’s direction. There will be two backhauls (UWB and 802.11), and every device’s position is known. Build a spanning distribution structure:

  • the gateway sends over UWB to N L2 relays;

  • each relay sends over 802.11 to M L3 fixtures, with N × M ≥ the mesh;

  • the cooperative algorithm that builds it must survive moving scatterers (people) and moving fixtures.

The flood stays as the bootstrap and the fallback. Code: firmware/mesh/src/tree.rs (state and decisions) and firmware/mesh/meshsim/src/dist.rs (the packet-level comparison).

Choice: an RPL-style DODAG with a two-tier objective function.

Objective. A node’s rank is rank(parent) + w(radio) · ETX(link).

  • Radio weight. UWB links weigh 0.5 and 802.11 links 1.0. The root’s UWB neighbours therefore become the L2 tier on their own. Where neither end has UWB, the same rule builds a two-tier 802.11 tree.

  • Consolidation. Joining a parent that is still a leaf costs +0.5, as in HEED. Fixtures prefer existing relays, so N stays small.

  • Child cap. A parent advertises its child cap M: C6 8, P4 and Pi 12. Those are the class targets of 8/16/32, clamped so that the children plus 4 candidate parents fit in the 16-slot neighbour table.

  • The cap is soft. Joiners skip a parent that is full. Joiners that race on a stale count can overshoot, and children with no other eligible parent stay. Measured: the worst excess is 4, and with the full-MAC data plane (below) a relay’s child count costs no airtime.

  • No shedding. Shedding excess children was tried and removed: at 32 fixtures it raised parent changes from 1.7–5.3 to 4.3–7.4 per node-minute without bounding the excess.

Links and the neighbour table.

  • ETX is kept per neighbour and per radio, from advert reception (1 s windows, EWMA ¼). 802.11 adverts come once a second, as do UWB beacons (one TDMA slot per node per second).

  • Position priors. Localization gives a prior ETX for a link with under 3 windows of history: 802.11 good within ~8 m, UWB within ~15 m line of sight. A neighbour that moved more than 2 m has its history reset.

  • Table admission (CTP-style). The parent, children and the root are never evicted. A newcomer takes a free slot, or displaces the worst-ranked unprotected entry, but only if it names us as parent or ranks better than that entry. Otherwise it is ignored.

  • Why admission is needed. The first version admitted everyone by evicting the stalest entry. From 32 fixtures more neighbours were audible than the table holds, and it evicted about 2,000 entries per node-minute. Every parent loss in that version was an eviction.

Stability and repair.

  • Hysteresis. A node switches parent only for a gain of ≥ 0.5 ETX, and at most once per 5 s.

  • Lost parent. A parent is lost after 3 s of silence, or after 2 s of advertising no route. The 2 s hold-down stops a relay’s brief repair from cascading through its subtree.

  • Local repair. A lost parent resets the node’s rank, so any non-child neighbour can take over. A neighbour that names us as parent is never chosen (no 2-cycles); otherwise loops are avoided by rank.

  • A defect the tests caught. Before the rank reset, a node whose only alternative had its own old rank stayed on a parent without a route indefinitely.

Data plane.

  • Root. The root sends each update on UWB (its data slot every 4 ms; 80-byte frames, so a packet is fragmented and every fragment must arrive) and on 802.11.

  • Relays and leaves. Relays rebroadcast once on 802.11 (0–2 ms jitter). Leaves never relay. Orphans fall back to the managed flood.

  • Full MACs. Every packet carries every member’s per-receiver MAC entry (§7.9), exactly as in the flood, so any overheard copy delivers.

  • Subtree MACs were measured and rejected. Each packet carried only the forwarder’s subtree entries. That made the tree a single-path structure: at 32 fixtures it delivered 87–94 %, against 99.99 % with full MACs (64 fixtures: 63 %).

  • Reliable commands (1 per second in the model) carry a sequence. A node that sees a gap (a later sequence, or its parent’s advert naming a newer one) asks its parent by unicast NACK. Live updates are not repaired: the next one supersedes.

  • Relay cache. The model keeps 16 reliable packets per relay. On a C6, 4 × 200 B = 800 B covers 4 s of reliable commands at 1 Hz (an estimate, not measured).

Control overhead. The tree adds 12 bytes (TreeAd) plus the position (4 bytes) to adverts that exist anyway (§5.3). It also adds NACKs, repairs, and a child report from each relay every 5 s. Measured: 22–53 B per node per second.

Security is unchanged. The tree only decides who forwards. Per-receiver MACs and freshness are end to end (gateway → member), so a relay can drop packets but cannot forge or replay them. A malicious relay is an availability problem. Full MACs limit it: any other relay’s copy, or the flood fallback, delivers.

Any node can be the gateway, so there is one DODAG per root. The extra state per additional root is the rank, parent and role; the neighbour table is shared.

6.2 Mesh time¶

Mesh time must not depend on any single peer (owner, 2026-11-17).

  • Every BEACON carries the sender’s estimate of mesh time at its TX instant (t_mesh). A receiver pairs it with the hardware RX timestamp (RxMeta.rx_us, §4.1). That gives one sample per neighbour: the neighbour’s offset from this node’s clock, with an error bound from timestamp jitter, the sender’s own uncertainty and the fit residual.

  • Robust aggregation (firmware/mesh/src/timesync.rs, wired in by M4):

    • Samples from all neighbours heard in the last window are intersected with Marzullo’s algorithm. With n ≥ 2f + 1 sources and at least n - f of them agreeing, up to f liars cannot move the estimate.

    • Without such a quorum the estimate falls back to the median at low confidence.

    • Per-peer rate is still fitted over a 5 min window, rejecting outliers by residual, like TimeDiscipline.

  • Steps and slews:

    • A correction larger than 50 ms is applied as a step only at high confidence and with at least three agreeing peers.

    • Everything else slews, at most 500 ppm. A node that sees a single peer (low confidence) can be moved only 0.5 ms per second, which MeshStatus reports.

  • Effects keep running whatever mesh time does. The renderer’s clock never stops or waits; a correction only shifts where future apply_at deadlines land.

  • No root is elected. A gateway with an app session does not get to define time either; it is one source among its neighbours.

  • Error per hop: the TX instant is the time just before ns_mac_send, so queueing and pacing add jitter. Each beacon carries the previous beacon’s measured TX-to-air correction if the hardware exposes a TX timestamp (M3a looks for one). Expect tens of µs per hop.

  • AP-anchored bonus: members associated to one AP all hear its beacons, whose TSF is a common clock (reference-broadcast sync). The fit can use AP beacons as extra observations.

M4 on hardware (mesh-m4.md): two-step completion stamps (no hardware TX timestamp), two-way over the beacons, RX stamps on the MAC’s µs counter (0x600AD000): three C6s agree within 47 µs; a scheduled change lands within 0.76 ms (2 ms under BLE coex), always on the same frame. The show epoch rides in the time beacon (outside the admin port).

6.3 Mapping the show clock¶

  1. The app syncs with the gateway as today (TimeSyncPing/Pong, web/src/net/clocksync.ts) and sends the gateway SetTimeBase{epoch_ms} in the gateway’s clock.

  2. The gateway converts it to mesh time and floods TIME_BASE{epoch_mesh_us, generation}.

  3. Each member converts mesh time to its own millis() and applies the epoch through the same internal path as SetTimeBase. The bridge’s slews (≤ 2 ms steps) become new TIME_BASE generations, flooded the same way.

  4. apply_at_ms is ms on that epoch, the same number on every member. So a relayed SetUniforms{apply_at_ms} lands on the same frame everywhere, unchanged.

Lead: apply_at must be later than the slowest delivery. One hop with relay delay is under 25 ms (0–3 virtual slots of 5 ms plus airtime); three hops under ~75 ms; a missed broadcast plus one want-ack retry adds 1 s. The default lead stays the bridge’s 150 ms for one or two hops, and 300 ms when the target set is three hops out. A member that receives a change after its apply_at applies it on arrival and counts it late (reported in MeshStatus).

6.4 Live updates: as fast as the airtime allows, smooth on every member¶

Live moves (fader drags, uncommitted uniforms) should feel immediate on every fixture (Q13).

On the mesh (MeshStack::send_live, M2):

  • Coalesced per frame. A new live update replaces one not yet sent, so the mesh always carries the newest value at whatever rate its airtime allows. Live traffic never queues behind itself.

  • Compact and batched. One packet carries several uniforms, each as uniform id + 16-bit value, plus a sequence number (8 B for two uniforms). Deltas against the last committed scene keep it small. The per-receiver MACs (8 B per member, §7.9) dominate the size, not the values.

  • Priority over bulk. Live control relays at high priority; bulk is evicted first (§10.6).

  • Rate per mesh size. The app offers updates at its UI rate (60 Hz or more); coalescing turns that into the rate the mesh carries.

7.4 Open mode: what guests can and cannot do¶

The point of open mode: someone shows up with a fixture they built, it joins and starts doing the coordinated effects. A guest must be able to follow the show, and must never be able to run it.

Guests can:

  • take mesh time and the show epoch (TIME_BASE), so they land on the same frames;

  • follow the open groups’ scenes: the effect (fetching the blob), committed uniforms, brightness, crossfade;

  • follow live uniform moves (fader streams) on open groups;

  • relay follow-stream frames (more coverage for other guests);

  • send GuestHello and a small status every 30 s.

Guests cannot: send CONTROL to anyone, change membership or groups, originate scenes, effects or time, act as a gateway for management, or read member-to-member traffic (it is under the mesh key, which guests don’t have).

How the follow stream is protected. Guests hold no secret, so a symmetric key can’t prove anything to them. Two layers:

  1. Framing under a public open key, K_open = HKDF(mesh id, "open"), using the same envelope (§5.1) with a reserved epoch. It gives integrity against corruption, not authenticity: anyone can compute it.

  2. Owner signatures on what lasts. At mesh creation the app generates a mesh owner key pair (P-256; the private key stays in the app and the bridge, the public key goes to every member and is in the beacon). Scene commits, effect blob ids and TIME_BASE generations on the follow stream carry an ECDSA signature from the app; the gateway only relays them. A guest verifies them against the owner key it learned when it first followed (trust on first use, shown in the app as “following ”).

  3. Live moves are unsigned (a signature per fader frame is too costly). Guests accept them only for the signed scene’s effect, only within each uniform’s manifest range, and only up to the rate limit. A forger can wiggle a guest’s sliders between commits; the next signed commit puts it back. Members never act on open-key frames.

7.5 Frame protection, counters and freshness (members)¶

Implemented in firmware/mesh/src/{envelope,replay,security}.rs.

  • AES-128-CCM over every member frame (§5.1), keyed by HKDF(mesh key, epoch, "frame") salted with the mesh id. It reuses the netstack’s CCM (firmware/netstack/src/ccmp.rs, on the C6’s AES accelerator). M2 made that CCM generic over the tag length, streamed the AAD and compares tags in constant time; RFC 3610 vector #1 and an equivalence test with the old code cover it.

  • The counter never rolls over. Each sender has a 64-bit logical counter. The nonce uses all 64 bits; the short header sends the low 32.

    • A receiver rebuilds the high bits from the highest counter it has seen from that sender (SRTP’s rollover guess: the candidate nearest the last one). A wrong guess makes the nonce wrong, so the MIC fails. A guess can lose a frame; it can never accept a forged or replayed one.

    • Tests cover 2^32 boundaries in both directions, reboot jumps, and wrong guesses.

  • Persistence: blocks reserved ahead.

    • The device persists a high-water mark (every counter below it may have been used) one block of 2^16 frames ahead. The next reservation is asked for when half the block is spent, so the write lands long before it is needed and nothing stalls.

    • At boot the counter jumps to the stored mark. A counter is never reused, and flash sees one small write per 65,536 frames plus one per boot.

  • Replay window, independent of time. Per source, the receiver keeps the highest counter, a 32-frame bitmap and a synced flag: 16 B per source, 64 members = 1 KB. It checks before decrypting and commits only after the MIC verifies, so a forged frame cannot move the window.

    • A source the receiver has no state for (after a fresh join, or the receiver’s own reboot) is learned from its next long header. That is only group-key authenticated, so the source is unsynced. Its security-relevant traffic (admin commands, membership, rekeys) is held until a freshness exchange syncs it: the receiver sends a nonce; the sender returns its current full counter, bound to the nonce by a MAC under their pairwise key (§7.9).

    • After a sync, only higher counters are fresh. A sync also undoes an insider who inflated a sender’s counter with group-key frames to starve it.

    • As a fallback, receivers persist their high-water marks occasionally and restore them at boot as a floor.

  • MIC: 8 B on 802.11; 4 B on UWB.

  • This replaces the swarm’s per-message HMAC for Splanc traffic.

    • Every other member switches as soon as it hears any frame under the new key. Only key holders can make one.

    • The old key stays accepted for 60 s after each node’s switch.

7.7 Groups¶

  • Group ids are u8 (0 = none). Names live in the app and the hub config; members store ids only.

  • AssignGroups{node, groups} (membership port, authenticated) sets a member’s groups, persisted.

7.8 How this relates to device trust¶

  • The app still pins each device’s certificate by MAC (deviceTrust.ts, trust.ts). A member reports its certificate hash in MeshStatus and JoinRequest; the app pins it at first sight and warns on a change, as for a direct connection. In locked mode the allow-list entry is that same device key.

  • The app-to-gateway leg is TLS or BLE as today. Gateway-to-member traffic is protected by the mesh key, not TLS. A member trusts the gateway because the gateway holds the mesh key.

  • The mesh owner key is new: it lives with the app’s site data (backed up with it, web/src/store/backup.ts) and is copied to the bridge when one manages the site.

7.9 Administration: kiosk-style unlocking, insider-resistant authority¶

The radiolarians’ mesh is closed (the site secret admits members), and administering it is unlocked with an admin secret, the way kiosk mode works. Kiosk mode today (docs/design/kiosk-mode.md):

  • firmware: firmware/player_app/kiosk_ffi.rs (lm_kiosk_gate classifies every arm before any handler; kiosk_unlock{secret} unlocks only the connection that sent it, until it drops; secret_eq is constant time; a wrong secret locks unlocking out for 2 s), persisted in /lfs/kiosk.cfg;

  • app: web/src/halloween/kioskSecrets.ts generates a 128-bit secret per device, keeps it in localStorage (splanc.kioskSecrets.v1), and web/src/ui/screens/kioskSection.ts offers “Copy owner secret”; the key is in the app backup (web/src/store/backupFormat.ts).

The hole in the first draft. The first draft used a shared HMAC admin key K_admin held by every member. Every member could therefore verify, and so forge, admin commands; and any holder of the group mesh key could impersonate any member. The design now gives no member a key that can create authority.

How TESLA works here.

  • Key chain. The gateway draws a one-way chain K_N → … → K_0 (K_i = F(K_{i+1}), SHA-256 truncated to 16 B; N = 65,535 intervals of 20 ms, 21.8 min) and commits to K_0.

  • Tag and disclosure. A packet sent in interval i carries an 8 B tag under a key derived from K_i. K_i is disclosed in every packet from interval i + d on (d = 3). The trailer is chain id, interval, tag, disclosed interval and key: 30 B.

  • Member side. A member buffers a packet only if, by its own clock, K_i cannot be out yet (the security condition). When a later packet discloses the key, the member checks that it hashes back to a key it trusts, checks the tag, and delivers.

  • Why an insider cannot forge. It learns K_i only once every member’s window for interval i has closed. In meshsim an insider forging with a disclosed key reached 0 of 9 members.

  • The clock bound comes from challenge-response, not mesh time. Owner rule: replay protection must not depend on time. A member sends TeslaMsg::Req (a nonce) and records its local send time t0. The gateway answers TeslaMsg::Sync: the chain commitment, its interval and the offset into it, MAC’d under the session’s pairwise key and bound to the nonce. The gateway’s clock at local time t is then at most gateway_at_reply + (t − t0)(1 + ρ), with ρ = 200 ppm.

    • An attacker who delays the Req or the Sync only makes the bound more conservative: packets fail the condition and are dropped, never wrongly accepted. There is a test for this.

    • No peer’s clock is involved, and an eclipse of mesh time (§6.2) cannot open a forgery window.

  • Replays. Rejected by the condition itself (an old interval’s key is public) and by the per-chain sequence floor.

  • Per-receiver MACs stay below 24 members (the same 24/16 hysteresis as the tree), for node-list targets, and for reliable commands, which are admin traffic and need no TESLA delay. A gateway falls back to them until its chain is built.

Apply-then-verify (Verifier::early, StackConfig::live_early, on by default):

  • Rendering. A member renders a live update the moment it arrives, if it passes the security condition. It verifies the tag when the key is disclosed, d intervals later. Genuine updates are then confirmed silently.

  • A failed tag. The update was forged. The member raises Event::LiveRejected, the app returns those uniforms to their last verified values, and the member verifies first (TESLA latency) for 10 s.

  • Bounds on what a forger can do:

    • Provisional updates are ordered by interval, not sequence. A forger can claim only an interval in the safe window, never a future one (refused), so the next genuine update overwrites its value.

    • Live moves are bounded by the signed scene and each uniform’s manifest range (§7.4), and the interpolator smooths them (§6.4).

    • Only live moves are applied early. Commands, scenes, membership and time stay verify-first or per-receiver.

  • Adaptive d (Q24) still matters, but now it sets how long a forgery can stay up, not the latency. Members make three challenges per chain, keep the tightest bound, and report their best round trip in the Req; the gateway rebuilds its chain with a larger d when one needs it. Measured: d settled at 4 × 20 ms or 6–7 × 10 ms with one rebuild.

Containing an insider that floods forged live moves (Q25, firmware/mesh/src/forensics.rs).

Apply-then-verify bounds each forgery, but a persistent forger keeps members verifying first, and at 30 Hz crowds their verify buffers: delivery falls to 68 % while it lasts. The goal: detect and contain the forger, and never let one liar get an honest fixture blocked.

Design: reports to the gateway, judged by quorum.

  • Reports. A member that verifies a forgery reports it to the gateway. The report is MAC’d under the pairwise session key, so every report is attributable. It says which neighbour the first copy came from, whether that neighbour’s RSSI fit its usual level, and whether that neighbour is the member’s tree parent.

  • Sampling. Members report only forgeries whose interval key falls in a 1 in 8 sample. The gateway accepts only those (it holds the chain). So every member reports the same forgeries, the airtime is bounded, and nobody can flood the gateway’s case table. The forger cannot avoid the sample: the key is unknown when it forges.

  • Provable lies. The gateway recomputes each reported packet’s tag. A report about a genuine packet is a provable lie and is dropped.

  • Suspects. A neighbour is a suspect for one forgery if at least 2 side witnesses name it. A side witness’s copy came from a neighbour at its usual RSSI that is not its parent; a forwarder’s children prove nothing about where a forgery started.

  • Its own account can clear it. That holds only if the upstream it names was seen sending the forgery by someone else, and following the upstream claims never leads back to it:

    • an honest relay names the injector and is cleared;

    • an injector that names a downstream relay is led back to itself;

    • an injector that names a node which never sent the packet has no witness for that claim.

  • Block and alert. A fixture that is a suspect in 3 forgeries within 30 s is blocked: the gateway orders every member to drop all live packets under that id for 60 s, and alerts the admin. Only the admin removes a fixture (rotation, §7.6).

7.10 What remains unresolvable¶

  • An eclipsed node (its only neighbours are malicious) can be desynced and starved.

    • Its mesh time moves at most 500 ppm on a single peer’s word (0.5 ms per second).

    • Its traffic can be dropped, and it can be kept from syncing a session.

    • It cannot be made to accept a forged or replayed command. Commands need the gateway’s per-receiver MAC, and freshness is counter-based, not time-based. Forged frames fail the MIC (outsiders) or the pairwise MAC (insiders).

  • A replay inside the freshness window right after a reboot. A command captured at most 64 of the gateway’s frames before the member’s sync (about 1 s while it streams control, up to about 30 s when idle) is accepted once.

  • Group-key data traffic (status, replies, bulk) can be sent by any member under any source id, and an insider can inflate a sender’s counter to starve it until the next sync. These are availability problems, not authority ones. Per-sender pairwise sync for non-gateway peers is staged for M7.

  • Node id ↔ device key binding is trust on first use at each gateway (an Ack for a node id already bound to another key is refused and reported). An admin-signed roster certificate per member would close this; it is staged for M7.

  • Approval joins trust the first wrap they receive (the joiner has no secret to check it against), as the app’s device pinning does today.

8. Gateway: one connection, the whole mesh¶

8.1 New protocol arms¶

In ledmapper.proto (arm numbers from 99 up; the exact numbers are chosen in the PR):

message MeshTargets {
  repeated uint32 nodes = 1;   // empty and group == 0: all members
  uint32 group = 2;
  bool include_gateway = 3;    // dispatch locally too (default true for "all")
}

// ClientMessage arm: relay one ClientMessage to mesh members.
message MeshSend {
  uint32 op_id = 1;
  MeshTargets targets = 2;
  bytes inner = 3;             // an encoded ClientMessage from the allow-list (§5.4)
  bool want_reply = 4;         // collect per-member replies
  uint32 timeout_ms = 5;       // default 2000
}

// ServerMessage arm: the aggregated result of a MeshSend.
message MeshResult {
  uint32 op_id = 1;
  repeated uint32 acked = 2;
  repeated uint32 missing = 3; // no reply before the timeout
  repeated MeshMemberReply replies = 4;  // when want_reply: small replies only
}

// Membership and listing: GetMesh / MeshState (members, groups, mode, pending joins),
// CreateMesh, JoinMesh (direct), AdmitDevice, SetGroups, SetMeshMode, RotateMeshKey.

8.2 Behaviour¶

  • Fan-out: the gateway checks the inner arm against the allow-list, dispatches locally if targeted (lm_ws_dispatch), and floods one MeshControl. One broadcast reaches every target; per-member unicast is only for a short explicit node list.

  • Replies: each target sends MeshReply unicast to the gateway with the op_id and a status (ok, error code, or a small reply under 150 B). Large replies (welcome, effect uniforms of a big effect) are not relayed; the app uses MeshStatus and targeted get_* calls whose replies fit.

  • Aggregation: the gateway collects replies for timeout_ms and returns one MeshResult. For live control (seq set, a fader stream) it does not wait: it acks the app on its own dispatch, like ControlAck, and reports stragglers in MeshStatus.

  • Listing: the gateway keeps the latest MeshStatus of each member (64 × ~60 B ≈ 4 KB; on a C6 gateway this is the largest new RAM cost, so it may keep a compact 24 B subset instead). GetMesh returns it.

  • Any fixture is a gateway: whichever one the app or bridge is connected to (owner’s decision). There is no gateway role in the mesh itself; a fixture with no session is just a member, and two admins on two fixtures are two gateways at once.

  • P4 + C6 boards: the C6 front end runs the mesh layer and forwards control for its own P4 through the existing link (frontend_link.cpp), so a P4 fixture is a normal mesh member.

11. UWB backhaul (later)¶

  • Hardware: no current Splanc board has UWB. The C6 boards, splanc-mini and the P4 + C6 design (docs/design/p4-c6-split.md) have no UWB radio.

  • Board change: add a Qorvo DWM3000 module (DW3110; what the swarm uses) or a QM33120 (two antennas, PDoA/AoA) on SPI, with IRQ, RST and WAKE: about 6 GPIOs. On the C6 that competes with LED outputs and the IMU pins (6, 7, 21 in docs/design/sensor-core.md), so it is a new board revision (the atopile boards under hardware/). On P4 + C6 boards the UWB part could hang off the P4 instead.

  • Rates: 6.8 Mb/s PHY, 125 B frames. The swarm’s TDMA gives each node ~2.5 ms of spare airtime per 30 ms superframe (~6 frames); its design estimate is ~19 KB/s per hop for pages, and it moved a 450 KB image to 3 nodes in ~3.5 min. Enough for control and effects, slow for Splanc’s 2 MB images.

  • Software: the swarm’s TDMA, DS-TWR and radio loop are C++ in apps/common/radio.cpp; the driver is GPL-derived. A Splanc UWB backhaul implements Backhaul (§3.2) with TDMA driving may_send, and needs a C6 port of the driver.

  • What it adds: ranging between every pair at ~20 Hz (§12), a backhaul on a different band from Wi-Fi and BLE (no coex), and µs-level time sync from DS-TWR timestamps.

  • As a distribution tier: §5.8 uses UWB as the gateway’s first hop to the L2 relays, with 802.11 below them.

13. Ranging over the C6’s own radio (research track)¶

An independent track. Nothing in the mesh depends on it.

13.1 Stock BLE ranging on the C6: no¶

  • Espressif’s ESP-IDF v6.1 feature table for the ESP32-C6 controller lists Channel Sounding: unsupported and AoA/AoD: unsupported. The host stacks list Channel Sounding as experimental, but a host can’t sound without a controller that supports it.

  • What remains with the stock controller is RSSI: meter-level error indoors, worse with multipath and bodies. Usable for “which fixture is nearest”, not for placement.

  • Our BLE host (firmware/blehost) runs over the vendor controller’s HCI, so it inherits these limits. The #166 bench work (memory note “amd-rig SDR BLE PHY RE”) showed how far from-source BLE radio control is: the RF TX key is set in a timer ISR we could not trace, and from-source BLE transmission never radiated. A self-built BLE CS through the BLE controller is a dead end for now.

13.2 Wi-Fi FTM: a hardware RTT that our stack bypasses¶

The C6 supports 802.11mc FTM (initiator as a station, responder as an AP), with an erratum: revisions v0.0 and v0.1 cannot be initiators (WIFI-9686). FTM is typically 1–2 m indoors. It runs inside the vendor Wi-Fi stack, which our netstack replaces, so using it means either reverse-engineering its fine timestamps or a vendor-stack ranging mode. Noted as a fallback, not planned.

13.3 What a self-built CS needs¶

Bluetooth Channel Sounding has two measurements:

  • Phase-based ranging (PBR): the initiator sends an unmodulated tone on channel k; the reflector measures its phase, then sends a tone back that the initiator measures. The product of the two measurements cancels both LO phases, if each device’s LO stays phase-continuous between its RX and TX on that channel. Stepping over up to 72 channels at 1 MHz (~78 MHz span) gives the distance from the slope of phase against frequency: unambiguous to ~150 m, with decimetre-level precision in line of sight.

  • Round-trip time (RTT): needs TX/RX timestamps at ns resolution. The C6’s RX timestamp is in µs (300 m per tick), so RTT is out unless the FTM timestamp hardware is reverse-engineered.

So the question is whether the C6 can (a) emit a clean tone at 1 MHz steps, (b) capture coherent I/Q of a received tone, and (c) keep LO phase across a TX/RX turnaround.

13.4 What the public reverse-engineering work gives us¶

  • Raw I/Q capture (RX): ESP-SDR (ESPARGOS, GPL-3.0) found an undocumented “dump engine” in the modem, used by Espressif’s factory RF test (librftest.a, adctrig). It copies baseband samples into SRAM: signed 10-bit I and Q plus the gain index in each 32-bit word, at 80, 40 or 16 MS/s, up to 16,380 samples (~205 µs at 80 MS/s). The C6 is supported: capture memory 0x40840000, SRAM owner register 0x60095004, dump control 0x600a9004, lane select 0x600a9014 (their main/targets/esp32c6/chip.h). Tuning off the Wi-Fi grid goes through phy_set_freq(mhz, offset_khz), so 1 MHz steps are possible. It needs the vendor PHY initialised, which our netstack already does before it takes over the MAC.

  • Tone TX: the C6 PHY library has the RF-test tone functions (ESP-SDR calls ram_stop_tx_tone; the start counterpart is in the same library), and espdr-tx steers the PHY test tone in frequency and amplitude on the ESP32-S3 to transmit voice. ESPARGOS states that arbitrary, even phase-coherent, transmission is possible but does not publish it. I did not find public raw I/Q write for the C6; the owner mentions such work exists, so it is an open question (§17) where it lives and whether it covers the C6. A CW tone is all PBR needs, so the test tone may be enough.

  • Our own MAC/PHY work: the netstack’s register map (firmware/netstack/src/regs.rs) and the #166 bench (a C6 cabled to a HackRF One through 40 dB, with a demod oracle) are where the spikes run.

13.5 The real risks, after the I/Q path¶

With RX phase available, the risk moves to:

  1. Phase continuity across a turnaround. PBR needs the LO phase on channel k to be the same when a device receives and when it transmits. ESP-SDR re-calibrates on each new frequency and waits 3 ms after selecting an LO. If the TX/RX switch re-locks the PLL or re-runs a calibration, the phase is random per step and PBR fails.

  2. LO and sample-clock coherence between two C6s. Separate 40 MHz crystals (±10–40 ppm) give up to ~100 kHz of carrier offset at 2.4 GHz. Each step must estimate it from the captured tone (a 205 µs capture gives ~5 kHz bins; phase-slope refinement goes below) and the two devices’ steps must be scheduled to µs-level (mesh time gives tens of µs).

  3. Capture length and step rate. 16,380 samples per capture is enough for a tone. But each capture hands a whole SRAM block to the dump engine: ESP-SDR’s C6 build reserves 0x40820000–0x40860000 (256 KB). A standalone C6 player has nowhere near that free. So on a C6 player this only works in a dedicated ranging mode, or on P4 + C6 boards where the C6 front end does not render and has the RAM.

  4. Hop latency. With a 3 ms LO settle, 72 steps × 2 turnarounds is ~0.5 s per pair. Fine for static fixtures, too slow for tracking.

  5. The radio is shared with the Wi-Fi link and BLE. A ranging session takes the radio off-channel; it must be scheduled like a background sweep.

  • Q15. Adaptive hop limit (§10.6).

  • Q16. Open mode’s budget accepted; trimmed to +912 B (§10.4).

  • Q17. The custodian spill stays, behind the custodian_spill feature, off by default (§10.4, §10.6).

Answered 2026-11-18 (Q18–Q21, the distribution tree):

  • Q18. Automatic: flood up to 16 members, tree from 24, 5 s hold in between (tree::Selector, §5.8).

  • Q19. The broadcast authenticator is built: TESLA with a challenge-response clock bound, above 24 members; per-receiver MACs below (§7.9). 128 fixtures at 30 Hz now deliver 99.4 %.

  • Q20. Multi-hop UWB: relays forward over UWB to their UWB children (§5.8). It cuts 802.11 airtime 13–23 % with the authenticator.

  • Q21. Every fixture may relay and caches 4 reliable packets (828 B, measured; tree state 1,596 B in all).

Answered 2026-11-18 (Q22–Q24):

  • Q22. Decided: adopt apply-then-verify for live moves above 24 members. It keeps live moves at p50 1–4 ms and p99 4–32 ms at 32–128 fixtures. A forging insider can show a fake value for ≤ ~0.1 s per attempt, then members verify first for 10 s (§7.9).

  • Q23. Accepted: any fixture can be the gateway (~3.9 KB on the C6).

  • Q24. Built: best-of-3 challenges, round trips reported, the gateway raises d with a new chain.

New (2026-11-18):

  • Q25. Decided: adopt gateway-quorum auto-quarantine (§7.9).

    • Detection: a forger is blocked mesh-wide in 2.5 s, with no honest fixture blocked by one liar. Delivery under attack is 95.5–96 % (68 % without), at 0.4–0.85 % airtime.

    • Residual: two colluders, or a forger impersonating a neighbour, can get an honest fixture a 60 s block; only the admin removes a fixture.

New (2026-11-18):

  • Q26. Decided: on a quarantine the app shows the evidence first, then offers removal: who reported, how many independent witnesses, timestamps, and whether the named fixture says it is being impersonated. The gateway sends MeshQuarantine (§7.9); the protest needs T5.
